<p>
<img src="images/igr-beans.png" width="100" align="left">
More of an epic saga than a paper, we recently completed a
gruelling comparison of numerical models to bursts observed back in 2009
from the 3.5-hr binary IGR&nbsp;J17511&mdash;3057. This work was the most
challenging yet for the 
<a href="https://github.com/outs1der/beans">beans(p)</a> code,
written by <a href="https://adellej.github.io">Adelle Goodwin</a> and myself,
due to the large number of bursts (21) to simulate.
</p>
<p>The results unequivocally point to a mass donor composition that is
remarkably hydrogen and metal (CNO elements) poor; only about 3% hydrogen
by mass, and 0.04% CNO elements. The corresponding "solar" composition
values would be 70% and 2%, respectively; such H-poor composition is
particularly unexpected given the relatively wide 3.5-hr orbit for this
object.</p>
<p><a href="https://docs.mesastar.org">MESA</a> models of the possible
evolutionary path by <a href="https://ryosuke-hirai.github.io">Ryo
Hirai</a> suggest a common
envelope phase in a fairly massive progenitor, that scoured away all the
H-rich outer material of the donor almost down to the He core. While there
are still many puzzles to explain the properties of this particular
object, we are finding an unexpectedly wide range in donor compositions
from such studies that challenge our conventional picture of the LMXB
population.
<p>The paper has been submitted to 
<a href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/publications-of-the-astronomical-society-of-australia">PASA</a> and is also available at
<a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18226">arXiv:2611.18226</a>. If you'd
like to read an explanation of the work in plain language (and multiple
languages), visit the nice summary at <a
href="https://gist.science/paper/2611.18226">Gist.Science</a>.
</p>
